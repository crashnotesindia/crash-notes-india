Seventy for five at Hagley Oval, and the Saturday I stopped switching off early

I have a bad habit with cricket on a weekend afternoon. When India lose five wickets cheaply, I switch to something else and check the score later, usually to confirm what I already decided. On Saturday 24 October, India were 70 for 5 chasing 181 at Hagley Oval, and I had the remote in my hand.

A long Saturday of cricket

It was a strange day for an Indian fan anyway. India A had started their unofficial Test against New Zealand A in Lincoln at 3:00 AM IST, about twenty kilometres from Christchurch. The 2nd T20I began at 12:30 PM IST, an 8:00 PM local start under lights, on the same ground as Thursday's opener. I had planned my lunch around it, which tells you everything about my priorities.

Before the match I had read a page on the second Christchurch T20I with the IST timings and the result added underneath, mostly to check the toss time. What I liked was the argument that the second game in three days on one ground is a different match, because both sides have seen how the pitch behaved. That sounded like commentary filler at first. By the end of the evening it looked right.

How the game went

India won the toss and fielded. Glenn Phillips made 70 off 43 balls and Bevon Jacobs 58, and New Zealand finished on 180 for 5. That felt gettable after India had chased 225 on Thursday. Then Mitchell Santner did what the preview warned about. He took 4 for 21, the ball gripped, and India were 70 for 5.

This is where I usually switch off. I did not, mostly because I was still eating. Tilak Varma batted like someone who had not read the scoreboard: an unbeaten 51 off 26 balls. Naman Dhir made 31 and Nitish Kumar Reddy finished 26 not out. India reached 181 for 6 with one ball left, won by four wickets and went 2-0 up in the series. Tilak was player of the match.

What I took from it

Two things. First, the preview had flagged Santner's economy and the possibility of spin gripping on the same square, and that is exactly where the game turned. Reading a page written before the match, then seeing the result added under the same URL a day later, is a good way to check whether a preview was saying something real.

Second, my switching-off habit is just impatience pretending to be judgement. A T20 chase with ten overs left and a set batter is not over at 70 for 5. I knew that in theory. Now I have a Saturday lunch to remember it by.

I watched the next game in Wellington on the following Tuesday from the first ball to the last, remote on the table.
