Wind off Waterloo Quay: what I watch for when India play a T20I in Wellington

Every time India tour New Zealand, my uncle asks the same question at lunch: why does this match look so different from the last one? This October the answer for the third T20I was one word, Wellington, and I spent the 12:30 PM IST start explaining it between bites of rice.

A ground that is not really a cricket ground

Hnry Stadium, which many of us still call by its old Sky Stadium name, is a multi-purpose arena on the waterfront. Rugby lives there. Cricket arrives on a drop-in pitch, grown somewhere else and lowered into place. That changes how teams prepare. In Christchurch, both early games used the same square at Hagley Oval, so by the second match everyone knew what it did. In Wellington the captains are reading a surface almost fresh.

Then there is the wind. Wellington is famous for it. A strong breeze decides which end a fast bowler wants, how far a mistimed hit travels, and whether a spinner can make the ball drift. I always watch who bowls the first two overs from which end. It tells you more about the captain's plan than any graphic.

The record that made me careful

Before the match I read a Wellington T20I preview that walks through India's defeat, tie and washout at the grounda>. Three games, no win in normal time. In February 2019 New Zealand made 219 for 6 and India lost by 80 runs. In January 2020 both sides finished on 165 and India won the Super Over. In November 2022 rain washed it out without a ball.

Three matches with different squads prove very little. What they do show is the character of the place: big first-innings scores on a flat strip, and weather that can stop everything. The preview also made a point I liked about betting talk after a 225-plus game. A big total at Hagley Oval is weak evidence for another big total on a different pitch in a windy city, and a line at 1.85 needs you to be right about 54% of the time just to stand still.

How it went

India won that game by six wickets with 40 balls to spare and went 3-0 up, so my uncle decided Wellington was easy after all. It was not easy. It was one evening when the conditions suited India's attack. The next time a drop-in pitch and a harbour wind turn up, I will still start with the same three checks: the wind direction, the first look at the strip on the pitch report, and whether the toss winner chooses to chase.

For viewers in India the match sat right across the working day, first innings through lunch and the chase ending around 3:50 PM IST. If you follow a New Zealand tour from here, block noon to four and keep your phone charged.
