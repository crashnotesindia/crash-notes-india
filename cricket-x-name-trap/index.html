A ball, a burst and a familiar word: Cricket X explained for cricket fans

Search for cricket games in India during a busy month and one name keeps coming up: Cricket X. Plenty of the people who click it are cricket fans who expected match markets, a fantasy team or a live score from a site they half remember from an IPL ad break. What they find is a crash game from SmartSoft Gaming with a cricket ball where Aviator has a plane.

What happens in a round

It works like most crash titles. You place a stake before the round, the ball flies, and a multiplier climbs with it for as long as the ball stays in the air, which might be a fraction of a second or long enough to make you nervous. Cash out before the ball bursts and you keep the stake times the number on screen. Wait too long and you get nothing. There are no batters, no bowlers and no match, and the cricket lives entirely in the artwork.

The numbers are harder to pin down

SmartSoft's own page for Cricket X is short. Really short. It describes the climb and the cash-out, the cricket theme and support for phones and desktops. It does not print a return to player, a maximum win or a bet range. That is unusual next to the same studio's JetX, whose specification sheet lists a return range of 96.2% to 98.9%.

Catalogues fill the gap with their own figures, and they do not all agree: SlotCatalog lists 98.8% and a top multiplier of 25,000x, while other explainers give a range from 96.7% to 98.8% depending on the build an operator chooses. Those are third-party numbers, and the information panel inside the game build is what counts. Aviator is simpler on this point, because Spribe publishes 97%.

Three names to keep apart

Start with Aviator itself. Some pages call Cricket X "Aviator cricket", which is a neat search phrase and a poor description, because the two are separate games from separate studios with separate maths.

A second mix-up involves cricket betting. Markets on an actual match, such as who wins the Wellington ODI or how many runs come in the powerplay, depend on the match. Cricket X depends on a random number generator and has nothing to do with any real game being played.

Then there is CricketerX, a later SmartSoft cricket crash game listed in catalogues from March 2025. The names are close enough that search results blend them. If a review quotes features or numbers, check which of the two it means.

Predictor apps

Telegram channels sell Cricket X "predictors" in the same way they sell Aviator ones. Crash results are fixed before the round begins, and no app, bot or screenshot service sitting outside the game can read them in advance, however confident the channel admin sounds. Anyone asking for money to share the next burst point is running a scam.
